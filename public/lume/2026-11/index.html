Phobos: the small moon that may hold the story of Mars

Launched in October, the MMX mission will try to bring at least ten grams of material from Phobos back to Earth. The sample could help distinguish between two very different stories about the origin of the Martian moon.

Phobos looks like little more than a dark, irregular rock, much too close to Mars. That modest appearance is precisely what makes it interesting. We do not know whether it began as an asteroid captured by Martian gravity or is what remains of a colossal impact on Mars. The two hypotheses predict different materials, ages and mixtures. An orbital photograph helps describe the surface, but a sample lets us measure atoms, minerals and organic compounds with instruments that cannot fit on a probe.

The Martian Moons eXploration (MMX) is a Japanese mission with European and North American participation, making the leap from images to physical material. It launched successfully on 20 October 2026. JAXA plans a mission of about five years, observing Phobos and Deimos, making two descents to Phobos and returning at least ten grams to Earth. ESA’s published schedule points to arrival in Mars orbit in 2027, surface operations in 2030 and the return capsule reaching Earth in 2031. These are mission targets, not guarantees: navigation, landing and the return itself remain risky phases.

Why it matters

A sample from Phobos could connect the history of small carbon-rich bodies with the evolution of Mars, including water and material ejected from the Martian surface. It would also test technology essential to future round trips to the Martian system.

Two origins, two signatures

The capture hypothesis says Phobos was a small body from the outer Solar System or asteroid belt that became trapped by Mars’s gravity. The impact hypothesis says the opposite: Martian material, perhaps mixed with the impactor, was thrown into orbit and gathered into a moon. Phobos’s nearly circular orbit, close to Mars’s equator, often favours an origin in a debris disc, but its dark reflectance and spectral similarity to carbonaceous bodies keep capture in the running. None of these clues settles the case on its own. Oxygen isotopes, hydrated minerals, carbon-rich grains and the proportion of Martian material in a sample could carry far more weight than a colour seen from a distance.

How to land where almost nothing weighs anything

Phobos has such weak gravity that the challenge is not just to land without breaking anything, but to avoid bouncing back off the surface. ESA estimates that the European Idefix rover, weighing 25 kilograms on Earth, will weigh the equivalent of about 15 grams there. MMX will first observe the moon from a quasi-satellite orbit, a trajectory that follows Phobos around Mars and allows many sites to be studied before one is chosen. The rover should measure the texture and composition of the regolith, helping prepare for the main descent. Only then will the mothership attempt to touch down, collect grains and rock, and pack them into the return capsule. Sampling is not a logistical detail: a contaminated, lost or overly altered sample loses some of its scientific value.

A mixed archive, courtesy of Mars

Even if Phobos is not a piece of Mars, its surface may hold a small Martian library. Impacts on Mars eject material into space; some of it may have fallen onto the moons over billions of years. JAXA describes the mission as a way to study the evolution of the Martian sphere and the origins of water and organic matter on terrestrial planets. That promise calls for caution: finding a Martian signature is not the same as finding life, and a surface sample does not automatically represent Phobos’s interior. But in laboratories on Earth, researchers will be able to separate grains, date events, look for volatile compounds and compare the chemistry with meteorites and Martian rocks. That is a measurement with incomparably higher resolution than an orbital spectrometer can provide.

Collaboration is an instrument too

MMX is led by JAXA and brings together NASA, CNES, DLR and ESA. Europe is providing the Idefix rover, which will scout the terrain before the main sampling attempt. The mission architecture is deliberately demanding: travel to Mars, operate repeatedly beside an irregular moon, descend safely and send a capsule back to Earth. Japan’s experience with Hayabusa2 helps, but Phobos is not the asteroid Ryugu: it sits in an environment dominated by Mars’s gravity and has its own geological history. If the chain works, the result will be more than an answer about one moon. It will be a concrete demonstration that material from the Martian system can be brought back for open, repeatable and independent analysis.

Open questions

Will the sample clearly distinguish capture from impact, or reveal a mixture that is hard to separate? What fraction of Phobos’s regolith came from Mars? And how did the moon evolve as its orbit slowly descends towards the planet? The answer will depend on the composition, the chosen site and whether the capsule makes it back intact.

The sea cucumber that intercepts carbon snow

In the midwaters around the Galápagos, Pelagothuria natatrix catches particles drifting down into the abyss. The observation suggests we should see the biological pump as a living chain, not a simple fall.

At first glance, it looks like a pink, almost transparent jellyfish suspended in blue. But Pelagothuria natatrix is a sea cucumber, an echinoderm related to starfish. It does something extraordinarily unusual for its group: it spends its entire life away from the seafloor, opening a gelatinous veil like a funnel in deep water.

That funnel catches what is known as marine snow: the slow rain of organic remains, cells, aggregates and particles leaving the ocean’s sunlit layers. A study by Bruce H. Robison, published in September in Ecology, brought together submersible observations in the Galápagos and found these animals to be abundant between 250 and 650 metres. The discovery does not prove that they change the climate on their own, but it makes a middle layer visible that simple carbon diagrams often leave out: before reaching the seafloor, carbon passes through many consumers.

Why it matters

The ocean’s ability to transport organic carbon into the deep depends not only on how much material sinks, but also on what intercepts it along the way. Mapping these consumers improves our understanding of the biological pump and helps distinguish local observations from global estimates.

A rain that starts at the surface

It starts with phytoplankton. In sunlight, these microscopic algae use dissolved carbon dioxide to produce organic matter. Some is eaten near the surface; the rest clumps together, dies or is packaged into faecal particles and begins to sink. It is called the biological pump because this transport can move carbon from the atmosphere and surface waters into the deep ocean for a long time. But it is not a sealed lift. The longer the journey, the more chances microbes and animals have to respire, fragment or repackage the material. What reaches the abyssal seafloor is only the surviving fraction of a chain of transformations.

The collector in the mesopelagic zone

More than 1,700 sea cucumber species live on the seafloor, where they collect organic matter that has already settled. Pelagothuria natatrix is the known exception: its tubular structures evolved into a membranous veil that helps it swim and feed. The animal points the veil upwards, facing the falling particles, and uses a crown of tentacles near its mouth to ingest them. Robison analysed 23 submersible dives in the Galápagos. Finding the animal repeatedly, and at relatively high densities between 250 and 650 metres, suggests it is not an isolated curiosity: it is a regular consumer in the vertical movement of matter through a zone we still observe poorly.

Diverting is not making it disappear

The study’s key word is “short-circuit”. By eating marine snow halfway down, the cucumber shortens the direct journey from surface to seafloor. The carbon it ingests may be respired and return as dissolved carbon dioxide, become biomass, be released as another kind of particle or pass to predators. Each outcome happens at a different depth and lasts for a different amount of time. So the cautious conclusion is not that this species has been measured as a new global climate pump. It is that models treating particle flow as a downward line may miss the ecology that changes it. In the tropics, where this animal has been observed, that difference may matter when estimating how much carbon escapes recycling in midwater.

What still needs measuring

Submersible images are both a strength and a limitation. They let researchers count fragile animals without destroying them and place them in the water column, but they do not directly measure, in every observation, how many grams of carbon each individual catches or the chemical fate of each meal. Abundance, size, feeding, respiration and seasonal variation still need to be linked to measurements of particle flux. We also do not know whether other tropical regions have comparable populations. The methodological lesson is that the deep ocean does not begin at the seafloor. To understand its role in the carbon cycle, we need to follow particles through the communities living between sunlight and the abyss.

Open questions

What is the actual rate of carbon intake per individual, what fraction is respired or exported again, and how widely does this Galápagos pattern occur in other tropical oceans? The study documents a plausible and important connection, not a closed global budget.

The beaver no algorithm can catch

Tiny Turing machines, enormous runtimes, and an AI that turns observed patterns into verified theorems.

Imagine a machine with six states, a head that only reads and writes zeroes and ones on an infinite tape, and a program compact enough to fit on one line. It sounds like a toy. In the Busy Beaver problem, though, some of these toys can run for such an enormous number of steps that simulation stops being an answer and becomes a wait with no guaranteed end. In general, knowing which machines halt is a question no algorithm can decide.

Why it matters

This is more than a curiosity about early computers. It is an especially hard test for mathematical AI: the system has to find a general description of behaviour, separate a conjecture from a proof, and let a formal verifier inspect the decisive part.

A maximum that grows faster than any recipe

For a fixed number of states, Busy Beaver looks among machines that eventually halt when started on a blank tape and asks which writes the most ones or runs for the most steps. The definition is simple, but the resulting function is not computable: if a program could give us that maximum for every size, we could use it to solve the halting problem. That would contradict Turing’s proof that there is no universal method for deciding whether any program halts. The irony is instructive: we can simulate any individual machine, but there is no procedure that always tells us how long is long enough. For small sizes, the community exhausts cases through enumeration, reductions and proofs. At six states and two symbols, some stubborn machines remain: the so-called holdouts.

From a chaotic tape to a short rule

The new preprint by Marco Dos Santos and colleagues uses these machines as a laboratory for autonomous discovery. First, the system simulates a machine and looks for recurring tape configurations, described with blocks and exponents instead of millions of individual cells. Then it asks a language model for a rule that jumps from one configuration to the next. This is an acceleration: instead of following every head movement, a compact transformation summarises many steps. Some rules have a Collatz-like flavour, with branches depending on parity that can expand or contract parameters. The delicate point is that observed data does not necessarily cover rare cases. A rule can fit every run we have seen and fail precisely at a boundary that only appears much later. So the team tests, refines and attempts an informal proof before formalising the claim.

Lean does not reward confidence

The final step is what gives the result substance. The authors translate the rules into Lean 4, a proof assistant that accepts only steps consistent with the definitions and theorems already available. The agent may discover that a boundary condition is missing or that a rule needs to be split, but the original Turing machine and starting configuration stay fixed. For an acceleration to count, every rule must be proved and the system must be closed: the initial configuration and every output must either halt or match another rule in the set. The paper reports new, verified accelerations for eleven machines in the BB(6) domain and makes the Lean files available in the Cryptids repository. This proves the long-range rules; it does not automatically resolve whether all those machines halt. The authors therefore use the provisional term “Cryptids” for behaviours described exactly but whose final destination remains open.

Discover, conjecture, prove

There is a healthy distinction to preserve when talking about AI doing mathematics. A simulation discovers a possible pattern. A conjecture gives it a general form and allows predictions. A formal proof checks that the form hides no exception, given the assumed definitions. The system described in the preprint goes through all three stages, but the strongest claim should be read precisely: the work was posted to arXiv on 19 October 2026 and is awaiting independent scrutiny. Whether it is genuinely new also depends on careful comparison with earlier community analyses. Still, the design is promising because it makes the division of labour auditable: generative models propose structures where brute-force search is blind, while Lean acts as a mechanical brake on elegant but false explanations. The gain may not be a final answer to Busy Beaver. It may be a new way to turn computational behaviour into verifiable mathematics.

Open questions

Will the accelerations now formalised be enough to decide whether any of these machines halt? Which parts of the method survive independent replication and other problem families? And how do we measure genuine discovery when a community already had clues or partial rules?

The coast the sea erased at Murujuga

In north-western Australia, stone tools and a spring now underwater help reconstruct landscapes where First Peoples lived before sea levels rose.

Some landscapes have not disappeared; they have simply changed element. Off Murujuga in Western Australia, divers and archaeologists are looking for a coastline that, in the early Holocene, still had rivers, hills and freshwater springs. Today it lies beneath the Indian Ocean. The question is not just whether there are objects on the seafloor, but how to show that they belong to an ancient human landscape rather than being stones moved by the sea.

The recent study connects stone tools, landforms and raw-material routes to a reconstruction of the continental shelf. It is demanding archaeology because the evidence has been filtered through thousands of years of waves, sediment and rising sea levels. And it does not begin with an empty map: Murujuga is Ngarda-Ngarli Country, cared for by Indigenous communities for at least 50,000 years.

Why it matters

Much of the coastline occupied during the last glaciation is now underwater. Finding credible ways to study it changes what we can map about early populations and reinforces that marine heritage management must include Indigenous knowledge and authority.

A moving coastline

Around 20,000 years ago, at the height of the last glaciation, enormous amounts of water were locked up in ice sheets. Mean sea level was much lower and the Murujuga shelf was exposed land. As the climate warmed, the sea advanced, flooding valleys and isolating high ground. The scale matters: the area now covered on Australia’s north-western shelf spans hundreds of thousands of square miles. This is not one lost archaeological site, but an ancient coastal region. To reconstruct it, researchers combine bathymetry, seabed geology and sea-level models. That lets them ask where freshwater, shelter, passage and workable stone might have been found before sending divers or survey equipment down.

When a stone becomes evidence

Stone is both the raw material and the problem. A flake may be the result of human toolmaking, but rocks also fracture naturally. Attribution does not rest on appearance alone: researchers look for a sequence of removals, striking platforms, edges and patterns consistent with a manufacturing technique. In Murujuga, one of the identified areas includes a freshwater spring and artefacts whose raw material has been linked to an island about 23 kilometres away. That transport is especially informative because it suggests choice and movement through an organised territory. Another site preserves a rocky rise facing an ancient river channel. The spatial arrangement connects objects, topography and resources, strengthening the interpretation of an inhabited landscape.

Diving is not dry-land excavation

Underwater, an object’s current position can mislead. Currents, storms, biological growth and deposited sand can bury, expose or move it. That is why underwater archaeology starts by documenting context: photographs and video with scale, coordinates, depth, sediment type and relation to the landform. Researchers then compare the assemblage with erosion models and known terrestrial sites. Confirmation may include petrographic analysis to identify the rock’s origin and dating of associated sediments or materials, where available. The result is rarely simple certainty. It is a chain of inferences, stronger when several lines of evidence point in the same direction and alternative interpretations are explicitly tested.

Heritage, knowledge and limits

Collaboration with the Murujuga Aboriginal Corporation is not institutional decoration. It shapes questions, names, access and responsibilities for a heritage that is still alive. The term “Country” brings together land, sea, ancestors and the duty of care, a relationship that the administrative divide between terrestrial and marine heritage does not always recognise. Still, this is a young and vulnerable field: not every site can be dated directly, and a tool on the seafloor cannot by itself reveal exactly when it was used or the full story of its maker. The new work’s value is making the search verifiable, not promising an intact archive. Every well-contextualised site helps protect part of the pre-inundation coast before construction, dredging or environmental change destroys it.

Open questions

What is the precise chronology of the different submerged assemblages, how many were moved after inundation, and how should legal protection be designed for a cultural landscape that stretches between land and sea? Future studies will need to combine dating, careful sampling and shared decisions with traditional knowledge holders.

The quiet market beneath our feet

Mycorrhizal fungi and plants trade carbon for nutrients without brains or contracts. The experiments show a real biological negotiation, not an altruistic forest.

A tree does not eat soil. To get phosphorus, nitrogen and water, it grows fine roots to the edge of what it can explore. There it meets a much more threadlike partner: a mycorrhizal fungus. Its hyphae, filaments just micrometres thick, enter soil pores the root cannot reach and connect with the plant at an interface where both exchange molecules. The plant sends carbon compounds made through photosynthesis; the fungus delivers mineral nutrients it has collected or released from the soil.

The popular image of a forest connected by a generous internet is appealing, but it confuses a physical network with intention. Evolutionary biologist Toby Kiers and colleagues describe this relationship as a market: participants with their own interests adjust what they supply according to the return. In a classic experiment with plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi, both fungi and plants directed resources towards partners that gave them the better reward. It is a negotiation without awareness, shaped by evolution, chemical transport and differential growth.

Why it matters

Mycorrhizas are among the broadest interfaces between plants and soil. Understanding who retains or returns carbon, and under what conditions, improves ecosystem ecology and avoids simplistic promises of sequestering carbon just by planting trees or adding fungi.

The currency changes hands at the interface

There are several kinds of mycorrhiza. In arbuscular mycorrhizas, common in many crops and herbaceous plants, the fungus enters the root cortex and forms arbuscules, highly branched structures that increase the exchange area without merging the cells of the two partners. The fungus can carry inorganic phosphorus and nitrogen to the root; the plant supplies sugars and lipids derived from carbon fixed from the air. In other plants, especially trees in temperate and boreal forests, ectomycorrhizal fungi wrap around root tips and grow between cells. The general rule is not a gift: each side pays a cost to obtain something it can get more effectively through the other. The outcome depends on the species, soil fertility, light and other microbes, not a universal formula.

Prices without a brain

Kiers and colleagues separated soil compartments in an experiment and varied the fungus’s access to phosphorus. When that nutrient was scarcer for the plant, fungi able to supply it received more carbon; in turn, fungi channelled more phosphorus towards roots that gave them more carbon. The 2011 paper calls this reciprocal reward. It does not mean a fungus calculates prices. It means local differences in molecular flows and growth favour the connections that return more. This observation is stronger than a metaphor: it measures flows and compares treatments. But it is also more limited, because a controlled pot does not reproduce the competition, diversity and seasons of a mature forest.

A network is not the same as cooperation

Hyphae can connect roots from different plants, and experiments have detected carbon or nutrient transfer through these networks. But the existence of that transfer does not show that one tree is helping another, or that the effect matters at forest scale. Material may move along physical gradients, through associated microbes or because the fungus favours whichever carbon source suits it best. Plants and fungi also conflict: a plant can reduce the carbon it gives to an unhelpful partner, and different fungi compete for the same root. The safest way to read the network is as an exchange infrastructure with evolutionary rules, not a moral community hidden in the soil.

Carbon in soil, with many exits

Some of the carbon a plant sends to a fungus becomes fungal biomass, compounds released into the soil or carbon dioxide respired back into the atmosphere. Another portion may contribute to soil organic matter, especially when microbial remains bind to minerals and are protected from decomposition. Comparative studies link the dominance of ectomycorrhizal trees with slower decomposition and larger stores of organic carbon in some soils. That does not justify a simple equation of more fungi with more stored carbon. Climate, mineralogy, land use, moisture and disturbance can reverse or reduce the effect. The useful scientific question is where, for how long and by what mechanism carbon stays, not just whether it entered a hypha.

Open questions

How much carbon transferred to fungi stays protected in soil for decades rather than being respired within weeks or months? How well do plant-fungus pair experiments predict natural communities with dozens of species? And how do these exchanges change when drought, fertilisation and warming alter the plant, fungus and soil microbes at the same time?

One sea, many wars

The Red Sea is no longer just the passage between the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean: it has become a place where crises from three regions feed into one another.

A ship leaving Suez for the Indian Ocean crosses a narrow corridor, but not a simple political boundary. Egypt, Sudan and Eritrea lie to the west; Saudi Arabia and Yemen to the east; Djibouti, Somalia and Ethiopia are only a short sail away at the southern end. For decades it was tempting to treat these shores as separate files. The Gaza war, Sudan’s civil war, Gulf rivalries and insecurity in the Horn of Africa changed that. Today, a decision in Sana’a can alter container routes, maritime insurance and diplomacy in Brussels.

Why it matters

The Suez–Red Sea–Bab el-Mandeb corridor links Europe and Asia along one of the world’s busiest trade routes. When passage becomes dangerous, diverting ships around the Cape of Good Hope adds time, fuel, emissions and pressure on supply chains. More importantly, safe navigation can no longer be separated from political stability on both shores.

The bottleneck that brings continents together

Geography explains the intensity of the problem. To the north, the Suez Canal concentrates the link to the Mediterranean. To the south, the Bab el-Mandeb Strait is the gateway to the Gulf of Aden and Indian Ocean. Between them, the Red Sea is relatively long and narrow, with ports, bases and submarine cables influenced by states facing very different crises. An incident does not have to formally close the route to cause economic damage: it is enough for shipowners, crews and insurers to consider the risk unacceptable. The International Maritime Organization has tracked attacks and disruption since late 2023 and reminds us that freedom of navigation and the safety of civilian crews are central obligations, not logistical details. The European response, Operation EUNAVFOR ASPIDES, was created specifically to protect merchant ships, not to conduct a land campaign in Yemen.

From the Gaza war to the Horn of Africa

Houthi attacks on ships they say are associated with Israel or its allies gave the Gaza war a maritime extension. But reducing them to a simple arrow between Yemen and Palestine misses the wider architecture. Yemen is close to Saudi Arabia, draws Iranian attention and faces Djibouti, where foreign military facilities cluster. Further west, war in Sudan has displaced people and made ports and external support even more important. In Somalia and Ethiopia, disputes over sea access and port agreements add another layer of tension. The International Crisis Group calls this an interconnected theatre: weapons, funding, political partners and navigation risks move between conflicts once studied separately. That does not mean there is a single command. It means each crisis changes the incentives in the others.

Ports are power, not just infrastructure

A port offers revenue, supplies, jobs and a physical link to trade networks. It also offers influence: whoever manages terminals, fuel access, coastal surveillance or anchorages can facilitate trade, host foreign forces or pressure a neighbour. Djibouti is the most visible case, with several countries’ facilities near the entrance to the Red Sea, but it is not the only one. Berbera, Port Sudan, Assab, Jeddah and Yemeni ports are pieces in national strategies and Gulf investment. This competition is not automatically war. It can bring investment and useful connections. It becomes dangerous when port deals replace political negotiation, or when fragile authorities use external partners to survive. For Europe, the lesson is uncomfortable: escorting ships reduces a symptom; supporting political agreements, coastal institutions and humanitarian aid addresses the causes that make the corridor amplify conflict.

What a naval escort can and cannot do

Frigates, radar and protection teams can reduce the chance of a successful attack and reassure a shipping route. They cannot decide the war in Yemen, rebuild Sudan or resolve relations between Ethiopia and Somalia. There is also a legal and political limit: protecting navigation is different from turning a defensive mission into a regional intervention. Seeing the Red Sea as a system helps avoid two illusions at once: that everything has the same cause, and that each flashpoint can be isolated. The map shows connections; it does not erase local differences. Better policy combines immediate protection for people at sea, transparent navigation information and patient diplomacy along the shores.

Open questions

A temporary drop in attacks would not by itself resolve the wars and rivalries that made them possible. It remains to be seen whether external actors will coordinate diplomatic efforts across the Middle East and Horn of Africa, and whether coastal communities will have a voice in agreements on ports, security and reconstruction. It is also uncertain how long shipowners will keep making costly diversions after the risk appears lower.

The last lace loom

In Scotland, one factory preserves the craft of making patterned Nottingham lace on machines that require mechanical skill and human memory.

Lace looks delicate, almost still. But before it becomes a curtain, costume or stage fabric, it is the result of hundreds of threads coordinated in a noisy mechanism. In Newmilns, in the Irvine Valley, MYB Textiles keeps a family of looms running to produce lace and patterned madras. The company describes itself as the last remaining producer of this speciality in the valley, an industrial survivor whose crucial raw material is not only cotton: it is workshop knowledge.

The story is not simply that an old machine still exists. It still makes a product with customers, while depending on operators who know how to prepare, observe and correct it. The photo essay published by The Guardian this month made that work visible again. It is worth looking beyond nostalgia: the factory helps explain why a technology can remain relevant when its pace, precision and material language are not easily replaced.

Why it matters

The story shows that industrial heritage is not only about preserving buildings or displaying a motionless machine. When production continues, it preserves gestures, diagnoses and technical choices that rarely fit into a manual. It also helps explain why some cultural industries still seek materials made through slow, specialised processes.

It is not handmade lace, but it is not a print either

Nottingham lace is machine-made, but that does not make it a simple printed surface. On a lace loom, sets of threads form the ground while others draw the pattern, crossing in a mechanical sequence. The result combines open and dense areas, creating pattern and transparency in the fabric itself. “Nottingham lace” names a technical and commercial tradition linked to the mechanisation of lace-making in Britain; it does not guarantee that every metre was made in Nottingham. What matters here is the fabric’s architecture: the design comes from the relationship between threads, tension and movement, not from ink applied afterwards.

That difference explains why it is hard to reproduce. A photograph can copy the look of a pattern, but not its depth, drape or the way light passes through the gaps between threads. Those material properties matter in set design, costume and interiors. MYB describes its production as handcrafted lace and madras for interiors and screen productions, a contemporary use that gives the machinery an economic reason to keep running.

A machine only works within a network of knowledge

Calling a loom traditional can suggest that it works by itself, through inertia. It is the opposite. It takes preparation of the warp, setting and watching threads, interpreting flaws in the pattern and adjusting the whole system when tension drifts out of balance. In processes like this, an operator does not just watch an alarm: they read physical signals, such as a thread giving way, a repeat losing definition or a vibration changing. Some of that diagnosis can be formalised; some is knowledge built up beside the machine.

The company’s timeline helps explain the fragility of this chain. According to its own institutional history, Morton Young and Borland was founded in 1900 in the Irvine Valley. The company links the local tradition to Flemish weavers and the expansion of lace mills in Newmilns and Darvel. The same source describes a sharp contraction in the sector from the late 1970s, driven by international competition, insufficient investment and gaps in training successors. This is an explanation of survival, not an independent measurement of the whole market, but it identifies the central point: machines and people grow old together.

Why film still wants controlled irregularity

The advantage of a factory like this is not beating a large-scale production line on price per metre. It is offering small runs, specific patterns and a texture with real visual consequences. In side light, woven lace creates tiny shadows and variations that synthetic mesh or a print may not reproduce. Those are exactly the details that matter to set designers, costume makers and interior designers, even when the audience cannot name the technique.

There is a useful paradox here. The more cultural production works with digital images and post-production, the more some physical objects become valuable as material evidence in the frame. Fabric is not just a prop: it reacts to movement, light and the camera’s proximity. The Guardian’s report captures this continuity through photographs of the factory and says most of the business comes from film, theatre, television and entertainment. It is a journalistic source, so it should be read as a reported account of the moment, but it aligns with MYB’s own commercial positioning.

Preservation is not freezing

There is a risk of turning every old workshop into a fable of resistance. MYB’s continued operation does not prove that every historic technology should be kept, nor does it remove the costs of energy, maintenance or training. It does show that the choice between automation and preservation is rarely binary. A factory can use modern logistics and digital commerce while keeping a production core whose advantage lies in human skill.

When looking at a photograph of a loom, the more interesting question may be: which part of the object are we seeing? Iron, belts and threads are visible. The method of adjustment, the ability to recognise a flaw and the relationship with customers who know how to ask for a particular effect are the invisible infrastructure. If that knowledge stops being passed on, saving the machine will matter, but it will not be the same as keeping the craft alive.

Open questions

The word “last” needs care: MYB and the report refer to patterned Nottingham lace production on traditional looms in the Irvine Valley, not all lace fabric production worldwide. Independent figures on output, equipment age and succession plans remain unknown. The decisive question is whether there will be enough training and demand for the craft to be passed on rather than merely documented.
